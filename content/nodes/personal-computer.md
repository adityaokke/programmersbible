> **In one sentence:** In 1975, the Altair 8800, a computer kit built around a microprocessor, helped start the personal computer: a computer that one person could own and use.

## The world before

For a long time, computers did not belong to people. They belonged to large organizations. The Computer History Museum puts it this way: computers were made mainly "for military, scientific, government, and corporate users" with big needs and big budgets. They were in labs, universities and big companies, but rarely in homes or small businesses.

IBM was "the leading business computer manufacturer", says the museum. IBM's own history page says that, like many other companies at the time, IBM sold computers only to businesses.

Then the [[microprocessor]] arrived: a whole CPU (the part of a computer that follows a program's instructions) on one chip. The museum says Intel's 8080 microprocessor, from 1974, made it easier for individuals to build computers.

## What happened

### Hobbyists build their own

Many early personal computers were built by **hobbyists**: people who build electronics for fun, not as their job. They worked in garages, student rooms and basements. They often worked from **kits**: boxes of parts that you put together yourself. They formed clubs to share tips. One was the Homebrew Computer Club ("homebrew" means homemade), which had its own newsletter.

### Was there an earlier one?

Before 1975, a few small computers were already for sale. In 1986, The Computer Museum in Boston held a contest to find the first personal computer. The judges chose the **Kenbak-1**, designed in 1971 by John Blankenbaker. It was built before microprocessors, from many simpler chips. Only about 40 were sold. The title "first personal computer using a microprocessor" went to the **Micral**, from France, in 1973. It used Intel's 8008 chip.

### The Altair 8800 (1975)

In its January 1975 issue, the hobbyist magazine *Popular Electronics* put a new computer kit on its cover: the **Altair 8800**. It was made by a company called MITS. The museum says that within weeks, customers "inundated" MITS with orders. (To inundate means to flood.)

The Altair used the Intel 8080 microprocessor. The museum's timeline for 1975 says it sold for $297, or $395 with a case. It came with 256 bytes of memory.

In its basic form, you programmed the Altair with rows of switches on its front. You set the switches to one instruction in [[binary]] and stored it in memory. Then you did the same for the next one, until the whole program was in. The only output was a pattern of red lights.

Ed Roberts, a co-founder of MITS, invented it. The museum's catalog says that many people credit the Altair with starting the personal computer revolution.

### A new company: Microsoft

In 1975, Bill Gates contacted the makers of the Altair. He offered them software for BASIC, a programming language (a way of writing instructions for a computer). With this software, Altair owners could write their own programs in BASIC. MITS was interested, so Gates and his friend Paul Allen had to write the software. The museum says: "Microsoft was born."

### Ready-made computers (1977)

The museum says that before 1977, most home computers were kits that needed technical skill. Then more companies began to sell computers that came already built. In 1977, three of them appeared: the **Apple II**, the **TRS-80** and the **Commodore PET**.

You can see in them the parts from [[how-computer-parts-work-together]]. The TRS-80, for example, came with a microprocessor (the CPU), 4 KB of memory (about 4,000 bytes), a keyboard for input and a screen for output. For storage it used a cassette tape, a small tape like the ones once used for music. It cost $599.95. Its manuals assumed the user knew nothing about computers before. Tandy Radio Shack, the company that made it, sold 10,000 in the first month. It had expected to sell 3,000 in the first year.

Steve Wozniak designed the Apple II. Apple Computer, which he founded with Steve Jobs in 1976, sold it. It used a TV as its screen. The museum says "millions of Apple IIs were sold between 1977 and 1993."

### The IBM PC (1981)

On August 12, 1981, IBM showed its own personal computer, the **IBM PC**, at a hotel in New York. Don Estridge led the team that made it. IBM says it was priced at $1,565, and for that it had 16 KB of RAM (about 16,000 bytes) and no disk drive. Adding a screen, two disk drives and a printer cost nearly $3,000 more. It used an Intel 8088 microprocessor. Microsoft provided its [[operating-system|operating system]]: a program that starts when the computer starts up and manages the other programs. It later became known as MS-DOS.


## Why it mattered

The museum sums up the change: "Individuals could now afford and understand computers, which dramatically changed everyday life."

The museum says schools were "a prime target" for most personal computer makers. Apple gave away thousands of Apple IIs to schools. The museum says this gave "a new generation their first access to personal computers."

The IBM PC changed offices. The museum says it was "the first PC to gain widespread adoption by industry." Companies bought PCs in large numbers. Other companies made **clones**: copies of the IBM PC that could run software written for it. So many computers followed IBM's design that, in IBM's words, it "became a standard". IBM adds that "PC became a generic term": a name for any personal computer, not only IBM's.

Growing PC sales also created a fast-growing market for software (programs). It included spreadsheets (programs for working with tables of numbers) and word processing (programs for writing documents). The story of personal computing continues in the [[smartphone]] lesson.

## Recap

- At first, computers were made for the military, scientists, governments and big companies. The microprocessor made it easier for individuals to build their own.
- The Altair 8800 kit, on a magazine cover in January 1975, is widely credited with starting the personal computer revolution. Earlier machines like the Kenbak-1 (1971) also have a claim to be first.
- Ready-made computers in 1977 and the IBM PC in 1981 brought personal computers to homes, schools and offices.
